╭─ 1 of 4 · What a strong design covers ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╮
│                                                                                                                      │
│  A strong design isn't a list of facts. It covers five things: structure, focus on the user, named tradeoffs, real   │
│  numbers, and a decision you commit to.                                                                              │
│  "It depends" is a fine start if you say on what, then decide.                                                       │
│                                                                                                                      │
│  Think of it like: a contractor's quote. It says what you need, what it costs and what they'd do, not every option.  │
│                                                                                                                      │
│  Why a PM cares: Real design reviews use this same structure.                                                        │
│                                                                                                                      │
╰──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╯

╭─ 2 of 4 · The structure: 1 to 5 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╮
│                                                                                                                      │
│  1. The user and the job to be done: who it's for and what has to go right.                                          │
│  2. Requirements: quality, speed, cost, and how bad a mistake would be.                                              │
│  3. Model: the smallest one that meets the quality bar, and why.                                                     │
│  4. Settings: temperature or effort, matched to the task.                                                            │
│  5. Context: what the model must see, pasted or fetched.                                                             │
│                                                                                                                      │
│  Why a PM cares: Reviewers listen first for whether you start from the user, not the model.                          │
│                                                                                                                      │
╰──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╯

╭─ 3 of 4 · The structure: 6 to 9 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╮
│                                                                                                                      │
│  6. Cost: users × requests × tokens × price, per month and per user.                                                 │
│  7. Risks and safeguards: what goes wrong (often a confident wrong answer) and what catches it.                      │
│  8. Recommendation: commit. "I'd use X because Y. I'd switch to Z if W."                                             │
│  9. Measurement: evals before launch, live metrics after.                                                            │
│                                                                                                                      │
│  Why a PM cares: End on a number, a decision and a check. That's what makes it a PM answer.                          │
│                                                                                                                      │

╭─ 4 of 4 · A worked example ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────╮
│                                                                                                                      │
│  A travel site wants a three-line summary of guest reviews on each of its 50,000 hotel pages.                        │
│  1 and 2. Travelers decide fast. Summaries must match the reviews and be cheap. Nobody waits: they run weekly.       │
│  3 and 4. Haiku 5.5, because summarizing is easy work. Low effort.                                                   │
│  5. Paste that hotel's reviews: about 6,000 tokens.                                                                  │
│  6. Batch, once a week: about $76.07 a month (prices checked 2026-10-09).                                            │
│  7. Risk: an invented complaint. Safeguards: each point quotes a review, and readers can flag a summary.             │
│  8. Haiku in batch; switch to Sonnet if the spot checks fail.                                                        │
│  9. Each week, check 100 summaries against their reviews and watch the flag rate.                                    │
│                                                                                                                      │
│  Why a PM cares: Nine short lines, one number, one decision: aim for that shape.                                     │
│                                                                                                                      │

Design an AI feature for: Medical-note summarizer (illustrative usage)
Turns a clinic visit transcript into a draft note for the doctor to review.

Users: 100 a day, 15 requests each (1,500 requests a day).
Each request: about 7,800 tokens in (1,100 of them a fixed system prompt) and 610 out.
Reference material: 190,000 tokens of documents the answers may need.
It runs in the background: nobody is waiting for each answer.
Answers have to match facts the model must be given.
High stakes: a wrong answer can hurt a patient.

Take a moment: who is the user, and what has to go right? Then make your choices.
